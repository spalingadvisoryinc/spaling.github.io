When a Legitimate Business Can't Prove It Exists

November 16, 2026

A company can be legally incorporated, registered with government authorities, and successfully verified by one system, yet fail verification through another.

Michael Spaling · Founder & Principal Consultant

A company can be legally incorporated, registered with government authorities, and successfully verified by one system, yet fail verification through another.

I know this because it recently happened to my company.

What began as a straightforward business verification exercise became an interesting example of how automated processes can create unexpected challenges for legitimate organizations.

Verified. Then Denied.

I recently applied for access to OpenAI's Daybreak program, which provides eligible cybersecurity professionals and organizations with access to additional AI capabilities.

Before applying, I had successfully completed OpenAI's API Platform business verification through Persona.

That process required considerable information, including my provincial driver's license, federal Business Number, legal and operating names, corporate title, shareholder identities and ownership percentages, and photographs of my face from three angles.

There was some initial confusion between my Alberta Corporate Access Number and my federal Business Number, but once resolved, the verification passed.

My company was officially verified.

The subsequent Daybreak application required another verification, again involving Persona.

This time, the requirements were somewhat different. I provided the front and back of my driver's license, including its barcode, my company's Business Number, legal and operating names, and another set of facial photographs.

Unlike the first process, I wasn't asked for detailed shareholder information.

The result?

Verification denied. Three times.

Each submission completed successfully, but approximately five minutes later, I received an email advising that verification had failed.

The first attempt included my company's optional "Operating As" name. Thinking this might be causing a mismatch, I removed it and submitted again.

Denied.

For the third attempt, I changed the Business Number to include its CRA GST/HST program account suffix (RTXXXX), which the form also accepted. I obtained this information directly from the Canada Revenue Agency's website while logged into my business account.

Denied again.

Each attempt produced the same explanation:

Reason:

"We could not confirm your business in official business registry records."

That was it.

No indication of which registry was checked, what information couldn't be confirmed, or whether the issue involved the business name, registration number, address, or something else.

Enter the Humans. Briefly.

I contacted OpenAI Support and began working with a human representative.

I provided screenshots, timestamps, device and browser information, and details of the verification attempts.

Support confirmed that the API Platform and Daybreak verification processes were separate. They also explained that my Business Number appeared to pass its format check, but the overall submission failed during registry verification.

They couldn't identify the specific reason for the failure or override the decision.

I offered to provide my Certificate of Incorporation and other legal documentation.

Then things became rather entertaining.

An AI-assisted support agent joined the email conversation while I was already working with a human representative.

It began asking for information I had previously supplied.

I responded:

"Hello AI-assisted support. I'm well aware and already working with a human named REDACTED 🙂"

The AI acknowledged that REDACTED might still be handling the case and offered to continue helping.

It then asked for the same information again.

When I explained that the requested screenshot was already attached to the ticket, the AI replied that it couldn't access the screenshot contents and asked me to paste the information instead.

At that point, I stopped responding.

I was attempting to resolve an automated verification failure through a support process that had introduced another automated system, one that apparently couldn't access information already provided.

When Security Controls Create New Risks

Here's where this experience becomes particularly interesting from a cybersecurity perspective.

I followed the legitimate process to request access to Daybreak. I provided the requested identification, completed the verification steps, and engaged Support when things didn't work.

But after three unsuccessful attempts and no clear path to resolution, I'm now incentivized to explore alternatives.

That could mean running an open-weight AI model locally, potentially without the safeguards and restrictions imposed by a hosted provider.

And therein lies an unintended consequence.

A verification process designed to control access to sensitive AI capabilities may inadvertently encourage legitimate security researchers to seek alternatives that operate outside those controls.

I'm reminded of something Valve co-founder Gabe Newell famously argued about software piracy: piracy is fundamentally a service problem.

When legitimate access is unnecessarily difficult, people may look elsewhere.

The comparison isn't perfect, but the underlying lesson is relevant. Security controls need to consider not only the risks they prevent, but also the behaviors they unintentionally encourage.

Remember the Human

I don't know why my verification failed, and I'm not suggesting the decision itself was necessarily incorrect.

But I do know that my company successfully passed one verification process, failed another three times, and couldn't establish the reason despite providing documentation and engaging Support.

A security control that prevents legitimate access without providing a meaningful resolution path can create incentives to bypass the intended process entirely.

As we introduce more automation into security and governance, we need to consider those unintended consequences.

Technology should support good judgment, not make judgment inaccessible.

And above all, we should always remember the human.
